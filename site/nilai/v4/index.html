Two thousand years of Tamil poetry, and five hundred more.

Scroll, or press Watch

Tamil is one of the oldest languages still spoken.

Its poets were cutting letters into stone and writing on palm leaf more than two thousand years ago. Some eighty million people speak it today.

Its poets put their hardest questions into things.

A balance for justice. A parasol for protection. A raft across the sea of deeds. A lamp for the mind.

Now we are building minds.

Machines that weigh us, shelter us, speak to us and remember for us. The old poems already asked the questions we need: who levels the balance, whom the shade misses, who stands behind the voice. This page carries thirteen of them to 2526 and asks them again.

Whatever is heard, from whoever’s mouth, understanding is seeing the true substance of that very thing.

Now the fluent voice may be a machine’s. The rule holds.

Correspondence, never confirmation: the poets are credited with the question, not with our science. The Tamil is the corpus, the plain line is the graph’s reading, the italic line is ours.

Two poems, one balance.

A Sangam poet wishes a king the steadiness of a balance. A later poem makes a whole court the balance. The gold thread between them is a link: a note on what one verse does to another. It can develop it, qualify it, share its image, agree with it, or pull against it.

the graph

40 books. 20,606 verses.

Classical Tamil literature. Its oldest poems come in two halves: akam, the inner world of love and the household, and puram, the outer world of kings, war, giving and death. After them come the ethics books, the epics, and the devotional songs of the saints and Siddhars. Each cluster is one book.

the graph

1,856 links that held.

Every link proposed between verses in different groups was graded blind, and only those judged sound, meaning well-founded, were kept. Brighter threads carry more of them.

அறம்

Virtue and conduct · the balance that levels itself

A puram poet wishes a king that he lean to no side, like the beam of a balance. A Sangam long poem moves the balance from the king’s person to his court. The Kural then asks the balance to level itself before it weighs: judgment begins by clearing oneself.

பொருள்

Wealth, polity and the world · the parasol, and who falls outside its shade

A puram poet tells a king his tall parasol is not for his own shade but to shelter those who suffer. An akam poem turns that rule on a husband: did she fall outside your shade? The Kural ties the rain to just rule, and the akam poem answers that the rain can come while one wife is still wronged.

இன்பம்

Love · the hill that played every instrument

An akam poem makes a hill a theatre: wind in the bamboo is the flute, the waterfall the drum, the bees the lute, the peacock the dancer, and the woman alone cannot bear the music. A Sangam hymn keeps the same orchestra and gives it to the hill’s god. A later love book moves it to the forest in the rains, still tormenting the woman who waits.

Release · the raft across the sea of deeds

The Kural says those who reach the Lord’s feet can swim the great sea of births. A Jain epic says what drags the others under: their own deeds, which whirl them like thread on a shuttle. A Jain hymn says the sea is made of the swimmer’s own deeds, and the Lord’s part is to build the raft.

உடல்

The body and medicine · the boat, the mist and the lock

A Siddhar song says use the body while the boat lasts. The Naladiyar agrees: take the body’s fruit, for it passes like mist on a hill. A Thiruppugazh song calls the body a contraption of strings shut with Brahma’s lock, and names what its fruit should be: to belong to the god.
