## LINK ainkurunuru:332 -> ainkurunuru:333 type=DEVELOPS
Note: In 332 the beasts fail to tell him that parting is cruel; in 333 the birds fail to tell him that they themselves live paired.
Teaches: Read in order, the decade moves from the argument the beasts should have made by precept (parting is cruel) to the one the birds should have made by their own example (we live with our mates), so the wife tries both ways of restraining a man and puts each in a creature's mouth.

## LINK kalittokai:61 -> kural:230 type=DEVELOPS
Note: Kural 230 says death is sweet when giving is out of reach; in Kalittokai 61 the friend says the girl, who would find it better to die than live unable to give to suppliants (ஈதல் இரந்தார்க்கு ஒன்று ஆற்றாது வாழ்தலின் சாதலும் கூடுமாம்), would give him whatever he lacks.
Teaches: The friend's offer turns the Kural's maxim into a trap for the household: once the lover presents himself as a suppliant for grace rather than wealth, the giving ethic obliges the girl to yield.

### kural:230: Kural 230
Tamil: சாதலின் இன்னாத தில்லை இனிததூஉம் / ஈதல் இயையாக் கடை
Pope: Nothing is more unpleasant than death: yet even that is pleasant where charity cannot be exercised.
Marked reading: Nothing is more bitter than dying, yet even that is sweet when giving is out of reach.
Marker's nuance: The chapter's last ranking sets giving above life. Parimelazhagar's reason is that it frees one from a bodily burden no use to anyone. This is hyperbole about value, not advice to die, and Kalaignar softens it to a comparison of pains.

## LINK patitruppattu:61 -> purananuru:236 type=DEVELOPS
Note: Purananuru 236 is Kapilar's lament that Pari told him to stay behind and did not take him in death; Patitruppattu 61 has Kapilar, Pari gone to the land from which none returns (வாராச் சேண்புலம் படர்ந்தோன்), arrive at the Chera's camp drawn by a fame for giving without regret.
Teaches: Together they show the bard's two answers to his patron's death, the vow to be with Pari in the next birth and the journey to a new patron whose giving is praised in Pari's terms, so the Chera's praise is partly an elegy carried over.

## LINK kuruntokai:50 -> kural:1234 type=DEVELOPS
Note: Kural 1234: with her partner gone, her shoulders have lost their old beauty and fullness and her bright bangles slip. Kuruntokai 50: the shoulders he embraced have wasted so that the bright bangles slip past the wrist, adorned with loneliness.
Teaches: The couplet names the wasted shoulder and slipping bangle as the sign of separation; the poem shows what the sign was used to say, setting it against his town's ford adorned with flowers, so the body's loss becomes an accusation of a place that still flourishes.

## LINK ainkurunuru:317 -> kural:1248 type=QUALIFIES
Note: In both the woman's heart has followed the man who left (பிரிந்தவர் பின்செல்வாய்; இறந்தோர் தேஎத்துச் சென்ற நெஞ்சம்); the Kural calls it a fool, Ainkurunuru 317 asks the friend why it is so slow to come back.
Teaches: The Kural judges the heart's going as folly, but the Ainkurunuru woman takes the going for granted and worries about the heart's delay, so the same flight of the heart becomes, in the older poem, a second absence that mirrors the man's.

Marked reading: The friend tells the girl how she spoke to the hill lord, in whose land a rutting elephant, taking a flowering vengai for a tiger, gores its trunk and, unable to pull its tusk free, bellows through the clefts like the ten-headed demon king straining beneath the god's mountain: when he comes by night through the hard path, heedless of snakes and the dark and the hill, she who had drooped like a field without water, like a poor man's youth, like the old age of one who never kept to virtue, grows lovely at dawn like a field after rain, like the wealth of a gracious man and the gain of a capable one; has he a way to keep that beauty from fading and from slander? and now, having heard her grief told, the good hill lord comes, seeing the vengai in flower, to marry her.

Note: Kalittokai 143's woman asks why the king who guards the world's lives as his own does not guard hers; Purananuru 186 says the world lives by its king and the king must know he is its life.
