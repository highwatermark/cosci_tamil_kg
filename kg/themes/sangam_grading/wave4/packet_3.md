## LINK kuruntokai:210 -> purananuru:150 type=DEVELOPS
Note: Purananuru 150: Nalli, unnamed, roasts fat in his forest for a starving poet and gives him his own chain. Kuruntokai 210: the friend prices the meal owed to the crow that called a guest in the ghee of the herds of Nalli's forest.
Teaches: Holding the two together shows why Nalli's forest is the measure in the love poem: he was remembered as the host who fed a stranger in the woods unasked, so the friend, repaying a bird for bringing a guest, reaches for the most famous hospitality she knows and still calls it too little.

Marked reading: In a cloak torn like an autumn kite's dark wing, the poet lay at the foot of a jack tree, past caring for himself, when a strong-bowed hunter with a gem on his head and an anklet bloody from killing deer saw his weariness, made him sit as he rose to bow, roasted white fat on a fire he struck himself before his men caught up, and bade him eat with his kin; when the poet had eaten, drunk at the cool falls and made to leave, the hunter said a forest people have no other rare treasure and gave him the chain from his chest and the bracelet from his arm, told neither his country nor his name, and only from others did the poet learn that he was Nalli, lord of the hill guarded by Thotti.
Marker's nuance: The poem turns on what Nalli withholds, his country and his name, so the gift comes without the fame it usually buys, and the poet repays him by naming him in the last line. Marker's fields: akam/puram: puram; marker's thinai: paadaan

## LINK kuruntokai:344 -> akananuru:54 type=QUALIFIES
Note: In Akananuru 54 the returning man sees the milch herd hurrying home at dusk to the calves with the cowherds behind and wants to reach his wife and son; in Kuruntokai 344 the waiting woman sees the cows with dripping udders leave the herd for the village at dusk and says only the fortunate see their lovers return.
Teaches: The same dusk image of cows hurrying home to their calves is the man's cue to return when he speaks and the woman's reproach when she speaks, so the herd's homing is a fixed sign of the duty to come home that each side reads against the other.

## LINK kuruntokai:183 -> akananuru:34 type=SAME_IMAGE
Note: Kuruntokai 183: the wife wonders whether he will see the stag parted from its doe; Akananuru 34: the hero, seeing a stag feed and guard its doe, bids his chariot hurry home.
Teaches: The pair shows the stag and doe of the scrub working from both ends of a separation: the waiting wife hopes he will see a parted pair and feel her loss, while the returning hero is moved by a pair together, so the same creatures carry absence for her and return for him.

## LINK ainkurunuru:361 -> akananuru:99 type=DEVELOPS
Note: In Akananuru 99 the man leading the eloped girl through the waste likens the opening kongu buds to women's jewelled breasts and points to paathiri petals on the path; in Ainkurunuru 361 the girl wears a paathiri garland by the summer stream and her breasts and shoulders strike him harder than her eyes.
Teaches: Akananuru 99 makes the waste's flowers stand for her body to coax her onward, while 361 has her wearing those same flowers and her body outdoing them, so the eloping man's praise moves from comparing the land to her to letting her eclipse it.
