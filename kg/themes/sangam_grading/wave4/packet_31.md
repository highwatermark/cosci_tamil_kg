## LINK ainthinai_ezhupathu:62 -> ainthinai_aimpathu:45 type=TENSION
Note: Ainthinai Aimpathu 45: will the herons of the grove let his oath go unpunished? Ainthinai Ezhupathu 62: the heron of the grove saw his cruelty and does not know it. On a plain reading one makes the herons guarantors of his oath and the other finds the heron a useless witness.
Teaches: Held together, the two shore venbas show the heroine's trust in the grove's herons as witnesses of a secret oath collapsing once he has gone: the birds she counted on to hold him are the birds she then reproaches for seeing nothing.

## LINK kural:759 -> kural:773 type=TENSION
Note: Two claims about the sharpest edge (எஃகு). In 759 wealth is the sharpest steel for cutting an enemy's pride. In 773 the edge of valour is helping that enemy when he falls.
Teaches: Cutting and sparing are both called sharpness.

### kural:759: Kural 759
Tamil: செய்க பொருளைச் செறுநர் செருக்கறுக்கும் / எஃகதனிற் கூரிய தில்
Pope: Accumulate wealth; it will destroy the arrogance of (your) foes; there is no weapon sharper than it.
Marked reading: Make wealth; there is no steel sharper than it for cutting down the pride of enemies.
Marker's nuance: The target is செருக்கு and not the enemy's body. Parimelazhagar seizes on this: other blades can't cut an intangible. The verse is one of the chapter's few bald imperatives.

### kural:773: Kural 773
Tamil: பேராண்மை என்ப தறுகண்ஒன் றுற்றக்கால் / ஊராண்மை மற்றதன் எஃகு
Pope: The learned say that fierceness (incontest with a foe) is indeed great valour; but to become a benefactor in case of accident (to a foe) is the extreme (limit) of that valour.
Marked reading: They call fierceness great manhood; but when something befalls the foe, generous help is its cutting edge.
Marker's nuance: The image is exact: the kindness isn't an exception to valour but its sharpest point. Parimelazhagar's example is Rama telling the defeated Ravana to go and come back tomorrow, an epic illustration that the verse doesn't state.

## LINK paripadal:10:71-88 -> kural:1066 type=DEVELOPS
Note: Kural 1066 says nothing degrades the tongue more than asking, even water for a cow; Paripadal 10.87-88 has bathers who, seeing what they lack, do good works in hope before they speak the shame of asking (இளிவரவு கூறாமுன்).
Teaches: The festival bathers apply the Kural's horror of asking even to what they want from the river, choosing an offering over a spoken request, so the ethic against begging shaped how people framed a petition in worship.

### kural:1066: Kural 1066
Tamil: ஆவிற்கு நீரென்று இரப்பினும் நாவிற்கு / இரவின் இளிவந்த தில்
Pope: There is nothing more disgraceful to one's tongue than to use it in begging water even for a cow.
Marked reading: Even if one asks for water for a cow, nothing is more degrading to the tongue than asking.
Marker's nuance: A limit case with the worthiest cause and the smallest request, and it's still degrading. Parimelazhagar: virtue itself should be done by effort, never by begging. He also notes that நாவிற்கு confines the disgrace to the spoken word, and that this still exceeds every other disgrace.

## LINK thinaimozhi_aimpathu:33 -> kural:812 type=QUALIFIES
Note: Kural 812 asks what it matters whether one gains or loses the friendship of those who are friends while there is profit and leave when there is none; Thinaimozhi 33 calls the husband's conduct exactly such a friendship.
Teaches: The Kural can shrug at the self-serving friend because a friend can be dropped, but the wife cannot treat a husband with that indifference, since what he consumed was her youth, so the akam poem shows where the maxim's calm stops working.

## LINK ainkurunuru:92 -> kuruntokai:181 type=DEVELOPS

Marked reading: The friend tells the woman not to weep in reproach that the man who left them is without virtue while her brow pales and her arms waste: he assured them he would come back in the cool rains, when on the slopes of Thiraiyan's tall Venkatam hill the bull elephant breaks young bamboo shoots to feed the hungry calf of his soft-stepping mate, and the peacock, bathed in vengai pollen, calls its mate from the kurunthu branch; that is the season, and the rain has spread.
Marker's nuance: The comfort is built from his own words: the friend quotes the promise, scene and all, and then points to the sky. The elephant feeding his mate's calf is inside that promise, so the man himself had chosen the image of a male who provides.
