Marked reading: Aravanar says that the one who gave up his life on Manipallavam, because his steady heart never let go of the cow that had once guarded and fed him, was born from that cow's womb to the gods' delight, one alone for this jambu island, and he tells the season of the birth, a day of spring fixed to the Bodhi lord's own, when a rain of holy water and flowers fell over the whole earth without a cloud.

## LINK silappathikaram:17:27-28 -> kalittokai:104 type=DEVELOPS
Note: Both pledge a herd girl to the man who, unafraid of the black bull's fury, takes it (காரி கதன் அஞ்சான்); in the Kalittokai her kin give her to the catcher, in the epic no bull is penned.
Teaches: The epic keeps the bull-catch pledge word for word but drops the contest, so a herders' marriage custom survives as the names of dance roles in a rite against ill omen.
Gains: epic side: the pledge gains its origin in a real contest in which a girl's kin give her to the man who takes the bull | other side: the custom gains a later life, recited over girls as a ritual formula with no bull and no catcher

Marked reading: This girl, garlanded with honeyed flowers, will love the man who, unafraid of the black bull's fury, leapt upon it.
Marker's nuance: The pledge is the bull-catch formula of the herders (காரி கதன் அஞ்சான், as in Kalittokai 104), but here no bull is penned and no man leaps: the old woman names each girl by the bull her tamer would take, as the first of the dance's roles. The speaker is inferred from lines 41 to 44, where she sets the seven girls in order.
Marker's speaker: மாதரி (maadhari), to ஐயை (aiyai)

Teaches: Read together, the two poems show that வரிசை cut both ways: 121 wants the patron to rank the recipient, 140 praises a patron who ranked by his own greatness and so overpaid, so the measure of a gift could be the giver's standing as well as the receiver's worth.
