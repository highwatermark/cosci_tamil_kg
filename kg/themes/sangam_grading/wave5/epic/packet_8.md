Note: Thirikadugam names three seeds of the month's three rains: the priests' virtue, the king's just rule, and the wife who follows her husband; the Neelakesi gives its land three rains a month from its own known goodness.
Teaches: The three-rains formula, which Thirikadugam ties to the conduct of priest, king and wife, appears in the epic as a landscape convention that explains away the missing palai tinai, its moral causes left unspoken.

Note: The quatrain says that knowing people still do blameworthy things because of deeds done before; the epic calls Virai's drunken death and Tarai's fall the ripening of a cruel deed.

### naladiyar:108: Naladiyar 108

## LINK neelakesi:704 -> kural:380 type=QUALIFIES
Note: The Kural asks what is stronger than fate, which comes first whatever one devises; Neelakesi attacks a fixity that sets in advance what comes, how, how much and when.
Teaches: The couplet claims fate outruns human contrivance, but the Jain attack shows that the vulnerable step is the further claim that every particular of an outcome is already fixed, which the couplet does not make.
Gains: epic side: the verse gains a contrast showing that its target is the total fixity of every detail, not every Tamil teaching of fate's strength | other side: the couplet gains a measure of how far its claim stops short of the Ajivika determinism that the Jain debater thought refutable

Note: The Naladiyar answers a beautiful woman's appeal by dissecting the body into entrails, blood and bone and asking which part is she; the Neelakesi's sage meets a full display of wiles with one question, what are these to us.
Teaches: The Naladiyar's dissection is a method for one still tempted, while the sage needs no method; and since this beauty is a demoness's borrowed shape, the Naladiyar's question of where the woman is in the body has, here, a literal answer: nowhere.

## LINK manimekalai:0:51-68 -> kural:225 type=QUALIFIES
Note: Kural 225 ranks the strength of those who bear hunger below the strength of those who remove it; in the patikam the renunciant who begs with a bowl is the one who ends Kayachandigai's hunger-fire.
Teaches: The Kural sets the one who endures hunger and the one who relieves it as two kinds of strength in rank, while the epic gives both to one person, a begging ascetic whose bowl feeds others, so its Buddhist renunciation removes the Kural's ranking rather than falling under it.
